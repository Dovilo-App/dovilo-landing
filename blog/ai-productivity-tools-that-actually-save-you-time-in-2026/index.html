AI productivity tools: where the time savings actually show up

Search for AI productivity tools and you get lists ranked by feature count. What is harder to find is an honest answer to the question underneath: how much time does any of this actually save?

The measured answer is smaller than the marketing, and smaller than it feels while you work. That gap is worth understanding before you add another tool.

What the measured numbers look like

The best public series on this is the Generative AI Adoption Tracker built by economists Alexander Bick, Adam Blandin and David Deming, published through the St. Louis Fed.

By the second quarter of 2026, 39.2% of employed US adults had used generative AI for work in the past week. AI assisted 6.3% of all work hours. The share of work hours actually saved was 2.2%.

That last number is the one to sit with. Adoption is broad and rising. The measured time saved, across the whole workforce, is roughly an hour a week.

These are self-reported savings, so if anything they lean generous.

Why the gains feel bigger than they are

The sharpest evidence on the perception gap comes from METR's randomized controlled trial of experienced open-source developers.

Sixteen developers worked 246 real tasks in repositories they knew well. Each task was randomly assigned to allow or forbid AI tools. With AI, they took 19% longer. They had forecast a 24% speedup, and after the trial they still believed AI had made them 20% faster.

The researchers are careful about scope, and so should we be: senior developers, mature codebases they already knew deeply. Greenfield work and unfamiliar code may behave very differently.

But the measurement problem generalizes. The parts of AI work that feel fast are visible, and the parts that cost you are not. Waiting for generation feels like progress. Reading output to decide whether it is correct feels like reviewing, not like work you would not otherwise have done.

If you have never measured, your estimate of your own gains is a guess with a hopeful bias baked in.

The categories that hold up

Writing and summarizing. The most reliable category. Drafts, replies, documentation, meeting notes. The savings are front-loaded at the blank page, and the judgment stays with you.

Coding. The highest ceiling and the widest variance. Agentic tools can read a task, implement, run tests and iterate. The METR result suggests the gain depends heavily on context: how well you know the code, how well specified the task is, and how much review the output needs.

Meeting capture. Transcription and action items are solid. They work on input you already have, and they don't reduce the number of meetings.

Research and learning. The weakest case. Output arrives fast and still needs verification, which is work you would not have had before.

Notice what these have in common. AI is good at producing a first version of something you can evaluate quickly. It is worse wherever evaluating the output costs as much as producing it.

The cost nobody measures: handoffs

Here is where the time actually goes for most solo developers and founders, and no tool list covers it.

You write a task in your to-do app. You open the agent. You paste the description into the context. You watch it work. You go back to the to-do app and mark it done. Then you do it again.

Each step is small. None of them is what you would call work. Run that loop a dozen times a day and it is a meaningful share of your week, spent entirely on moving information between two tools that don't talk to each other.

This is also the part of the workflow where measurement is easiest. You can count the copy-paste steps. You cannot easily count whether the generated function saved you 20 minutes.

How to get real savings

Measure one week before you change anything. Not a feeling, a count. How many hours went to the task, and how many went to moving information around.

Start with your highest-volume repetitive work, not the most interesting. The interesting task is usually the rare one.

Attack the handoffs first. They are the part you can automate with certainty. Whether the model's output was good is a judgment call; whether you had to copy it by hand is not.

Check the output, not the speed. The METR result exists because the developers never measured. Fast and wrong is slower than slow and right, and it does not feel that way in the moment.

Expect single digits, not double. If you are seeing 40%, either you found an unusually good fit or you are estimating rather than measuring. Both are worth knowing.

Frequently asked questions

How much time does AI actually save at work? Across US workers in Q2 2026, self-reported savings came to 2.2% of total work hours, with 6.3% of hours AI-assisted, per the Bick, Blandin and Deming tracker published by the St. Louis Fed. Individual results vary far more than that average suggests.

Do AI coding tools make developers faster? Not always. METR's randomized trial found experienced developers took 19% longer on tasks in codebases they knew well, while believing they had been 20% faster. The researchers caution against generalizing to unfamiliar code or greenfield projects.

Why do people overestimate their AI productivity gains? Because the cost is invisible. Generation feels like progress, and reviewing output feels like reviewing rather than extra work. Without measurement, the estimate is a guess with a hopeful bias.

What actually saves the most time? For most solo builders, removing handoffs between tools. Those are countable and automatable, unlike the quality of a given model output.

How does an MCP server connect an agent to a task manager? It exposes a set of tools the agent can call directly. Dovilo's local MCP server, a Pro desktop feature, gives agents read and update access to a task list that is separate from your personal one, so tasks move without copy-paste.

Can I try this without paying? Dovilo is free on iOS and Android with unlimited tasks, categories and focus sessions. The desktop app and the MCP server are part of Pro, which has a 7-day free trial started in the mobile app.

The tools that save the most time are the ones that remove a step you were doing by hand, not the ones that generate the most. Measure a week, pick your highest-volume work, and connect the tools you already have before adding another.
